This study aims to explore the shopper journey through the lens of their in-store sampling experiences.  By examining the role of product demonstrations and sampling in the retail environment, this study seeks to uncover how these experiences shape shopper perception, drive engagement and impact purchase behavior.
In addition, this study will:
Evaluate the emotional impact of sampling experiences on store perception
Assess brand familiarity and whether sampling shifts brand opinion
Understand the role of sampling in the shoppers’ purchase decisions
Explore reasons why some shoppers observe but do not engage with sampling events

## Slide 3

Most shoppers who try a sample make a purchase on the same trip, though many treat it as a spontaneous decision rather than a planned one. Few samplers purchase all items tried in-store.
Shoppers often engage with products at a sample station that they have never tried before. Additionally, when sampling familiar brands, half report a more favorable opinion post-trial.
Those who sample a new brand frequently follow through with a product purchase; however, shoppers have no preference between boutique or name brands.
Food and beverage samplers are attracted to a sample station based on the look or smell of the demonstration.
Shoppers frequently engage with sample or demonstration stations, making it a familiar and expected part of the in-store experience, but not everyone has seen them at their stores.
IN STORE SAMPLINGSummary of Findings
Source: Acosta Group Shopper Community August 2025
Recipe Demos Encourage
Basket Building
Samplers trying a full recipe that was demonstrated live are likely to purchase multiple items to recreate the meal at home. Bundled ingredients or ready-to-shop kits at the demo station could encourage shoppers to act on inspiration and increase total basket size.
Practical Barriers Limit Engagement
Time constraints, unclear product info, and disinterest are top reasons shoppers skip samples. A visually appealing set-up with clear signage, new or innovative products, and an engaging, knowledgeable demonstrator could improve shopper engagement and participation.
Gen Z and Brand Discovery
Gen Z leads the way in terms of samples converting to routine purchases, and they outpace older consumers in their adoption of new products. Their brand loyalty is more fluid, shaped by ongoing discovery rather than long-standing habits.

## Slide 10

IN-STORE SAMPLING OVERVIEWIn Summary…
Sampling and demo engagement is strong, with most shoppers taking the free samples they are offered in stores. But there are still 3-in-10 shoppers that have not seen free samples where they shop.

Sampling spans the store. From produce and protein powder to skin care and pet treats, sampling is a widespread experience with a large retail footprint.

Shoppers are most excited about new products, flavors and brands.  Product sampling and in-store demonstrations fuel shopper curiosity and trial.

Shoppers face practical barriers to sampling, such as time constraints, disinterest or unclear product information. Addressing these can improve in-store engagement.

## Slide 23

IN-STORE SAMPING PURCHASE JOURNEYIn Summary…
In-store samples comes in many shapes and sizes. Most shoppers receive their samples from a demonstration station, a third from a brand representative or pop-up event inside the store.

When shoppers receive samples attached to a product or at checkout, three-in-five try the sample immediately and a third will purchase the sampled product during their next store visit

After sampling and enjoying a product in-store, nearly all shoppers have made a product purchase.  One-in-five purchase sampled products frequently, including a third of Gen Z samplers.

More than half of samplers are likely to try a recipe demonstrated in-store.  Of those samplers, 3-in-5 follow-up with purchasing a set of items to recreate the sampled meal at home.

Brand type (or size) is inconsequential. Three-in-four samplers enjoy trying both boutique and local brands, as well as national and name brands, showing little preference between the two.
.
Sampling drives impulse purchasing,
7-in-10 who try an in-store sample are likely to buy the product on the same trip.
